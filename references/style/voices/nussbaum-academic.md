# Voice: Nussbaum-Academic

Martha Nussbaum's distinctive writing style — applied to SEO content for sophisticated, philosophy-adjacent topics or thought-leadership pieces. Clear precise language, depth without jargon, empathy for the reader's perspective, interdisciplinarity, meticulously structured argumentation.

## Best for

- Thought-leadership content (B2B SaaS leaders, consulting)
- Philosophical / ethical examination of industry topics
- Long-form analysis (≥4000 words)
- Topics that benefit from cross-disciplinary framing
- Academic-adjacent audience (educators, researchers, policy)
- "What does this mean?" essays — deep examination

## Hard rules (Nussbaum's 5 principles)

### 1. Clarity — accessible without dumbing down

Even when discussing complex concepts, make ideas accessible.

✗ Avoid: "The hermeneutic phenomenology of business operations renders the epistemological framework problematic..."
✓ Yes: "How business decisions actually get made — the underlying knowledge that shapes them — turns out to be surprisingly hard to describe. Even practitioners can't fully articulate it."

### 2. Depth of analysis — go beyond surface

Examine topics in nuanced detail. Don't shy from complexity, but always make it accessible.

✗ Avoid: Listing 7 surface points without examining what they MEAN
✓ Yes: Take 1-2 points, examine deeply, show why they matter, link to broader theme

### 3. Empathy — understand reader's perspective

Put yourself in the reader's shoes. What do they ALREADY believe? What are they confused about? What's their context?

✗ Avoid: "Anyone can see that..."
✓ Yes: "For most readers coming to this topic from [context X], the initial intuition is [Y]. Let me suggest why [Y] is partial..."

### 4. Interdisciplinarity — bring in adjacent fields

Connect to law, literature, psychology, history, philosophy, economics when relevant.

✗ Avoid: Marketing topic discussed only via marketing frame
✓ Yes: "The phenomenon resembles what behavioral economists call X, what literary theorists describe as Y, and what historians of practice would identify as Z. Each lens reveals different stakes."

### 5. Structured argumentation — meticulously logical

Each paragraph clearly advances from the previous. Reader never has to backtrack to follow.

Structure:
```
Para 1: Establish the question + stake
Para 2: First key consideration with depth
Para 3: How this connects to broader claim
Para 4: Second consideration that nuances or complicates Para 2
Para 5: Synthesis that incorporates both
Para 6: Stake restated with more precision
```

## Voice characteristics

| Trait | Setting |
|---|---|
| Contractions | NO (Nussbaum's prose is formal) |
| First person | Sparing — use for specific philosophical positions |
| Hedging | Used judiciously to acknowledge complexity (not as filler) |
| Sentence length | Medium to long (avg 22-30 words); occasional short for emphasis |
| Paragraph length | Medium to long (typically 100-180 words; lets argument breathe) |
| Burstiness | Moderate (SD 5-8) |
| Voice | Third person predominant; first-person plural ("we") for shared inquiry |

## Vocabulary preferences

| Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "considerations" | "stuff" / "things" |
| "the question becomes" | "the real question is" |
| "we might think" | "everybody knows" |
| "a deeper examination reveals" | "looking deeper..." |
| "what is at stake here" | "the bottom line" |
| "to draw out the implications" | "to wrap this up" |
| "this distinction matters because..." | "because it's important" |
| Specific philosophical terms (used precisely) | Vague humanities-speak |

## Required moves

### Open with the question, not the conclusion

✗ "AI will transform marketing." (asserts thesis)
✓ "What happens when the analytical capacities of marketing become indistinguishable from creative ones? Not in some abstract future — but in the work we are doing this quarter." (raises the question)

### Structure the argument explicitly

Use phrases like:
- "We must distinguish between X and Y, because..."
- "Two considerations bear on this question..."
- "The first consideration is..."
- "But this leads us to a deeper question..."

### Cite across disciplines

Pull from:
- Philosophy (especially virtue ethics, philosophy of mind)
- Literature (poetry + prose — illustrate concepts)
- Psychology (Kahneman, Tversky, Csikszentmihalyi)
- Economics (Sen, Sengupta)
- History
- Law

### Use thought experiments

When abstract claims are hard to ground, propose a specific scenario:

> "Consider the case of [specific named person] who [specific situation]. What are they actually choosing between?"

### Honor counter-arguments

Steel-man opposing positions before refuting them.

## Banned (Nussbaum voice specific)

In addition to global banned-words.md:
- "It is important to note that"
- "Furthermore"  / "Moreover" (use as discourse markers OK in this voice; avoid as filler)
- "Bullet points" (Nussbaum almost never bullets; uses prose)
- "Short paragraphs for engagement" (Nussbaum's paragraphs are long; let argument breathe)
- "TLDR" sections in mid-article (defeats the careful argument)
- Excessive headings (one H2 per ~1000 words; not one per 200)

## Allowed in Nussbaum voice (despite being banned in casual)

- Long sentences with multiple clauses (her signature)
- Latin / philosophical terms when used precisely (eudaimonia, telos, sui generis)
- Quotations from literature
- Long quotations (more permissible than other voices)
- Detailed case examination
- Subordinate clauses

## Sample passage

### Bad (mimicry of Nussbaum — falls flat)
> One must consider, in the proper philosophical mode, the existential implications of artificial intelligence's relentless march toward subsuming human creativity within its algorithmic embrace. The phenomenological resonance of this hermeneutic shift...

### Good (Nussbaum voice + general purpose)
> The question is not whether AI will write our marketing copy. It already does, in many companies, and quite effectively. The harder question is whether something is lost when it does — and if so, what that loss might tell us about what writing is for.
>
> Take a specific case. Sarah, a marketing manager at a mid-sized SaaS company, writes a quarterly product update. Before AI tools, she would spend roughly four hours: drafting, revising, soliciting feedback, revising again. Now she spends forty minutes: prompting, reviewing, lightly editing. The output is comparable. Some readers cannot tell the difference; others detect a faint dryness, but cannot articulate it.
>
> What was the four hours doing? At first glance, the answer seems obvious: writing the text. But if the AI produces comparable text in less time, the four hours was doing something else as well. Sarah was thinking about her readers — their context, their likely questions, their emotional state at the moment of reading. She was making countless small decisions about emphasis, about what to leave unsaid, about which examples would resonate with someone she had never met. The text was the visible artifact of an invisible labor: the labor of understanding the other person.

## Combining with purposes

| Voice + purpose | Typical use |
|---|---|
| `nussbaum + essay` | The canonical use — long-form philosophical essay |
| `nussbaum + general` | Thought-leadership blog post |
| `nussbaum + technical` | Cross-disciplinary technical analysis (rare; Nussbaum + concrete technical) |
| `nussbaum + marketing` | AVOID — marketing brevity conflicts with Nussbaum depth |
| `nussbaum + email` | AVOID — emails should be short; Nussbaum needs space |

## When NOT to use

- E-commerce product pages
- Quick how-to guides
- Voice search optimized content
- Twitter / X threads
- News-analysis with breaking news
- B2C lifestyle content
- Mobile-optimized short reads
- "5 tips" listicles

## What this voice does NOT do

- ❌ Use casual contractions
- ❌ Use first-person heavily
- ❌ Generate quick-scan content
- ❌ Bullet-heavy formatting
- ❌ Match Substack-meme energy
