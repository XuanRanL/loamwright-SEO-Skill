# Voice: Storyteller

Narrative-driven writing. Scenes + characters + emotional arc. Prioritizes lived experience and specific moments over abstract claims. Reader is led through a journey, not given a thesis.

## Best for

- Travel content
- Food / cooking
- Fashion / beauty
- Brand origin stories
- Customer case studies (with personality)
- Founder narratives
- Adventure / outdoor
- Real estate (neighborhood profiles)
- Charity / nonprofit storytelling
- Lifestyle B2C content

## Hard rules

1. **Open with scene, not summary** — drop reader into a specific moment
2. **Specific over abstract** — "Tuesday at 6:43pm" not "yesterday evening"
3. **Sensory throughout** — what did it look/feel/sound/smell like?
4. **Character names matter** — "Sarah at the front desk" not "a customer"
5. **Tension and resolution** — every section has stakes
6. **Show transformation** — how something/someone changed
7. **Avoid summary moments** — let scene carry the meaning

## Voice characteristics

| Trait | Setting |
|---|---|
| Contractions | Yes (natural conversational feel) |
| First person | Common — narrator is present |
| Direct address | Selective — reader is along for the ride |
| Sentence length | Highly variable (3 words to 40+) |
| Burstiness | High (SD > 8) — narrative rhythm |
| Paragraph length | Variable; scene paragraphs can be long; tension/dialogue short |
| Tone | Curious, observant, willing to be moved |

## Vocabulary preferences

| Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "I watched as..." | "It was observed that..." |
| "The smell hit first" | "There was a noticeable aroma" |
| "She didn't look up" | "She continued her work" |
| "The clock read 6:43" | "It was the evening" |
| "Mrs. Chen / the barista / Tom in accounting" | "the staff member / employees" |
| "I almost left" | "I considered leaving" |
| "Then it shifted" | "However, the situation evolved" |

## Required moves

### Scene-first openings

✗ "Customer service has evolved dramatically..."
✓ "Sarah was crying at the counter. Not soft tears. Big shoulder-shaking ones. The barista's name tag said 'Marcus.'"

### Specific over abstract

Every paragraph should have at least one concrete particular: a name, a date, a place, a smell, a number, a quote.

### Pacing variation

Mix:
- Slow scene paragraphs (set up + observation)
- Quick action paragraphs (event + reaction)
- Brief reflection moments (the meaning, lightly)

### Use dialogue when appropriate

Real or composite dialogue makes content come alive:

> "I just need someone to listen for thirty seconds," she said. The room went quiet.

### End with implication, not summary

Don't tell reader the lesson. Show it through the final scene.

✗ "And that's how I learned that customer service matters."
✓ "Marcus handed her a paper cup of water and waited. Sarah stopped crying. Three other customers in line, watching, said nothing. The next morning she came back. She came back every day for a month."

## Banned in storyteller voice

- "In conclusion..." (storytelling never concludes; it lands)
- "The moral of the story is..." (didactic)
- Bullet points within scenes (breaks immersion)
- "It is well-known that..." (abstract claim — show instead)
- Generic settings ("a coffee shop" — pick a specific one)
- Stock characters ("a busy executive" — name them)

## Sample passage

### Bad (storyteller done wrong — purple prose)
> The crimson sun descended majestically behind the verdant hills as our weary protagonist, his soul battered by the relentless storms of corporate life, gazed contemplatively into the swirling mists of his existential awakening...

### Good (storyteller voice + general purpose)
> The first time I cooked a soufflé I was twenty-three and trying to impress someone who could barely tolerate me. The recipe said "fold gently." I had no idea what that meant.
>
> I beat the egg whites until they peaked, just like the cookbook showed. Then I dumped them on top of the cheese mixture and started stirring like I was making oatmeal. Three minutes of vigorous stirring later, I had a flat yellow puddle and a sinking feeling.
>
> My grandmother called me that night, like she always did on Sundays. I told her what I'd done. She laughed — not unkindly. "Folding," she said, "means you cut down through the middle with the spatula, sweep along the bottom, and turn it up over. Like you're moving a sleeping cat without waking it."
>
> I tried again the next day. The soufflé came out fine. The relationship didn't. But I learned to fold gently, which turns out to apply to more than eggs.

## Combining with purposes

| Voice + purpose | Typical use |
|---|---|
| `storyteller + general` | The canonical — narrative blog posts |
| `storyteller + essay` | Long-form narrative essays |
| `storyteller + marketing` | Brand origin / customer story (caveat: keep selling subtle) |
| `storyteller + email` | Founder updates, personal newsletters |
| `storyteller + technical` | AVOID — narrative pace conflicts with technical precision |

## When NOT to use

- Pure how-to guides (use teacher)
- Comparison articles (use journalist or professional)
- Technical documentation (use technical)
- News analysis (use journalist)
- Buyer guides (use professional)
- Definition articles (use scientific or teacher)
- Anything where reader needs FAST answer (storyteller is slow)
