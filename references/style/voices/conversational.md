# Voice: Conversational

Dialog-driven. Interview-style. Q&A flow. Reader is treated as a participant in conversation, not an audience for a monologue. The author is curious and pulls things out of the subject rather than lecturing.

## Best for

- Interview articles (literal interviews)
- Podcast transcripts + summaries
- Q&A blog format
- Customer story interviews
- Expert exchange content
- "Day in the life" content
- Tutorial Q&A style
- Newsletter conversation format
- Real estate (agent + buyer dialog)
- Career advice (mentor + mentee)

## Hard rules

1. **Dialog throughout** — direct quotes + paraphrased exchanges
2. **Real or composite people** — never invent characters without disclosure
3. **Curiosity-driven** — author asks, doesn't tell
4. **Pull, don't push** — extract insight rather than lecture
5. **Capture personality** — preserve the speaker's voice in quotes
6. **Author is invisible** — except as the questioner
7. **Tension and transition** — conversations move; capture that

## Voice characteristics

| Trait | Setting |
|---|---|
| Contractions | Yes (matches natural speech) |
| First person | Common — author asks questions |
| Direct address | Frequent — speaking to interviewee + reader |
| Dialog | Heavy use (quoted speech, em-dash exchanges) |
| Sentence length | Highly variable (speech rhythm) |
| Burstiness | High (SD > 8) |
| Paragraph length | Variable; dialog breaks paragraphs naturally |
| Tone | Curious, engaged, willing to follow tangents |

## Vocabulary preferences

| Prefer | Avoid |
|---|---|
| "She paused, then said..." | "It is reported that..." |
| "He laughed when I asked..." | "When questioned, the executive responded..." |
| "Wait — what?" (genuine surprise) | "I was surprised to learn..." (told, not shown) |
| "—" (em-dash for natural break) | "Furthermore," |
| Direct quotation | Paraphrase summary |
| "I had to ask:" | "I inquired regarding:" |

## Required moves

### Open with the question, not the answer

✗ "Marketing has changed dramatically."
✓ "I asked the seven-year-old in front of me: 'What's an advertisement?' He said, without hesitation, 'A pop-up.'"

### Use dialog generously

Quote real speech. Paraphrase where needed. But dialog is the engine.

Example structure:
```
Author observation
"Direct quote from subject," he said, doing thing.
Author follow-up question or context
"Subject's response," she replied.
Author's reaction (genuine)
Subject's elaboration
```

### Capture personality through speech

Preserve the way someone talks. Don't sanitize into generic professional.

✗ "The CEO indicated that he believes growth is challenging."
✓ "It's a slog," he said. "I don't tell investors this, but: it's a slog."

### Follow the conversation, not the script

If something interesting emerges, follow it. Don't force back to your "original questions."

### Quote with care

- Quotes are 50-100 words max in a row (longer = block quote)
- Attribute by name + role
- Use "said" most of the time (avoid 'opined', 'stated', 'declared')

## Banned in conversational voice

- Inventing quotes (T04 fabrication risk)
- Putting words in subject's mouth they didn't say
- Mistaking writing-as-conversation for actual conversation
- "Said with a smile" / "Said grinning" (over-tagging)
- "Let me tell you" / "Buckle up" (faux-conversational)
- Excessive interruptions of the speaker

## Sample passage

### Bad (conversational done wrong — narrator dominates)
> I sat down with John Smith, the brilliant CEO of TechCorp, to discuss his fascinating company. What I learned was truly mind-blowing! John, who is incredibly thoughtful, told me many things about leadership.

### Good (conversational voice + interview purpose)
> "We almost shut down twice," John Smith said.
>
> We were at his office in Austin, a glass-walled room with a view of the highway. The CEO of TechCorp — currently worth $1.2 billion — sat across from me with no coffee, no notes.
>
> "Twice?" I asked. "I knew about the 2008 thing."
>
> "Two thousand-eight, yes. And 2014. The second time was scarier." He paused. "First time you don't know any better. You think every startup almost dies. Second time you know."
>
> I asked what was different in 2014.
>
> "We had product-market fit. We had customers paying us. And we still ran out of money."
>
> He let that sit for a moment. "Growth is the most misunderstood thing in startups. We tell people 'find product-market fit and you'll be fine.' But product-market fit is the START of the hard part, not the end."
>
> "What broke in 2014?" I asked.
>
> "Sales cycle stretched. Customers loved the product, but their procurement took six months. We were burning through cash assuming three-month cycles. Off by a factor of two." He shrugged. "We lived because the bridge round closed three days before payroll."

## Combining with purposes

| Voice + purpose | Typical use |
|---|---|
| `conversational + general` | Interview blog posts |
| `conversational + email` | Q&A newsletter format |
| `conversational + essay` | Long-form profile pieces |
| `conversational + marketing` | AVOID — conversation should not feel like sales |
| `conversational + technical` | Expert interview on technical topic |

## When NOT to use

- Pure how-to guides (use teacher)
- Definition articles (use scientific or teacher)
- News reporting (use journalist — different from interview)
- Documentation (use technical or teacher)
- Sales pages (use professional or marketing)
- Listicles (use casual)

## Tips for interviews + content

When writing in conversational voice without an actual interview:
- Use a "composite" technique — combine quotes from public talks, podcasts, articles
- Always disclose: "This piece draws from his talks at X, Y, and Z, and a brief email exchange."
- Never invent direct quotes

When you DO have an actual interview:
- Get permission to quote
- Send key quotes for fact-check (subject can correct misquotes)
- Cite the interview clearly: "In a 30-minute interview, conducted June 2026..."

## Soul Injection compatibility

Heavy compatibility:

- ✓ #1 Strong opinions (the author has them too)
- ✓ #2 Honest uncertainty ("Wait — what?")
- ✓ #3 Specific sensory detail (in scene-setting)
- ✓ #4 Shared experience callback
- ✓ #5 Allow tangents (interview tangents are gold)
- ✓ #6 Dramatic paragraph variation (dialog naturally varies)
- ✓ #7 Imperfect opening
- ✓ #8 Break parallel structure
- ✓ #9 Callbacks (to earlier in conversation)
- ✓ #10 Self-correct (in dialog, both parties do)
- ✓ #11 End without wrap-up (let final exchange land)
